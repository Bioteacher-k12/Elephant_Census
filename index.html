Populations of African elephants have declined at an alarming rate due to habitat destruction and poaching fueled by the international ivory trade. To measure how many elephants remained across Africa, researchers designed a standardized continent-wide aerial survey and compared two counting strategies:

Option A: Total Count

Flying over 100% of an entire region to count every single elephant. Accurate for small parks, but prohibitively expensive, slow, and dangerous across millions of square kilometers.

Option B: Sample Count (Aerial Strip Transects)

Survey regions are divided into sections called strata. Planes fly straight transect lines while observers count elephants inside two narrow counting strips (one on each side of the plane). Researchers calculate elephant density (elephants/km2) in the strips and extrapolate to estimate the total population.

Aerial Survey Protocol: Flight Path Blind Spot & Strip Width

Each transect has two counting strips—one on each side of the plane's dashed flight path—because observers looking out the left and right windows cannot see directly beneath the fuselage (the central grey corridor). Each counting strip is 2 km wide, so the combined counting width of a transect is 2 km × 2 = 4 km. Elephants standing directly on the dashed flight line or outside the shaded strips are not included in the strip sample count (though they are included in a full-landscape Total Count).

The Great Elephant Census: Regional Stability & Change

During the Great Elephant Census across 18 African countries, aerial observers counted both live elephants and elephant carcasses along transects. Scientists use the Carcass Ratio to determine whether a population is stable or shrinking.

Carcass Ratio Formula
Carcass Ratio = Dead Live + Dead × 100%
Threshold: > 8% indicates a declining population.
